# Citations checked 2026-10-29

Bibliographic details verified against the Crossref API (`api.crossref.org/works/<doi>`) on 2026-10-29; findings taken from the publishers' abstracts.

## 1. Du, Gao and Levi (2012)

Du, Qianqian, Huasheng Gao, and Maurice D. Levi. 2012. "The relative-age effect and career success: Evidence from corporate CEOs." *Economics Letters* 117 (3): 660–662. December 2012.
DOI: [10.1016/j.econlet.2012.08.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econlet.2012.08.017)

Finding: among S&P 500 CEOs the number born in June and July is disproportionately small, which the authors read as a relative-age effect (the youngest children in a school cohort, under the US cutoffs of their era, are less likely to reach the top job).

## 2. Covington, Swidler and Yost (2022)

Covington, Thomas, Steve Swidler, and Keven Yost. 2022. "Reexamining the relative-age effect and career success: new evidence from S&P 1500 CEOs." *Managerial Finance* 48 (1): 49–56. Published online 19 August 2021; issue dated 3 January 2022.
DOI: [10.1108/MF-02-2021-0090](https://doi.org/10.1108/MF-02-2021-0090)

Note: the DOI `10.1108/MF-04-2021-0183` given in the brief does not resolve on Crossref; the correct DOI is `10.1108/MF-02-2021-0090`.

Finding: using a larger CEO sample and exact state school-cutoff dates to compute relative age in months, the authors find no relative-age effect among S&P 1500 CEOs, either by season of birth or by relative age in months; the CEO birth-date distribution matches the US population and relative age has no effect on firm performance.

## 3. Bedard and Dhuey (2006)

Bedard, Kelly, and Elizabeth Dhuey. 2006. "The Persistence of Early Childhood Maturity: International Evidence of Long-Run Age Effects." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 121 (4): 1437–1472. November 2006.
DOI: [10.1162/qjec.121.4.1437](https://doi.org/10.1162/qjec.121.4.1437)

Finding: because a single annual cutoff makes the oldest pupils in a cohort about 20% older than the youngest, the youngest score 4–12 percentiles lower in grade 4 and 2–9 percentiles lower in grade 8 across OECD countries (TIMSS 1995 and 1999), and in the United States and Canada they are less likely to attend university.

Method note relevant to this project: Bedard and Dhuey determined each country's cutoff from the empirical distribution of birth months in TIMSS and confirmed it against Eurydice or the national education ministry; they excluded Australia, Germany, Hungary, Ireland, the Netherlands, Switzerland and the United States because the cutoff varied by region or was at educators' or parents' discretion. In their US natality analysis they dropped 13 states whose cutoff was mid-month or set by local education agencies (Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Iowa, Massachusetts, Montana, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New Jersey, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Wyoming, per their footnote 15, citing Datar 2003). Working-paper version: UC Irvine colloquium PDF, 2004 (https://www.economics.uci.edu/files/docs/colloqpapers/f04/Bedard.pdf).
